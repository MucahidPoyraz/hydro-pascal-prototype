What Is the Pressure Rating of a Hydraulic Cylinder?

22 MAR 2026

Of all the technical data on a hydraulic cylinder, the most misunderstood item is the pressure rating. It is often thought of as a single number; in fact, several pressure values are defined for a cylinder and each tells you something different.

Three Different Pressure Values

Working pressure (nominal pressure) is the pressure the cylinder can carry continuously and safely over its designed life. This is the value to use when choosing for a system.

Test pressure is the value applied in post-production testing and is above working pressure. The cylinder is expected not to leak at this pressure; but that does not mean it can work continuously at it.

Burst pressure is the value at which the cylinder is structurally destroyed. This value is a safety reference, not a working limit. Looking at burst pressure and thinking "so it can take this much" is one of the most dangerous calculation mistakes.

What Determines the Pressure Rating?

A cylinder's working pressure is determined by the weakest of several design elements. Body wall thickness is the main element resisting internal pressure. The cap connection may be threaded, welded or bolted, and each has a different strength limit.

Seal groove design is also decisive: at high pressure, the seal is forced to extrude out of its groove, and backup rings are needed to prevent this. Finally, the piston-to-rod connection must be able to carry the force transmitted.

All these elements are designed together. So a cylinder's pressure rating cannot be raised by changing a single part.

Staying Below System Pressure

When a cylinder rated below system pressure is fitted, no visible problem appears at first. The cylinder works and lifts the load. But in every working cycle the body is stressed beyond its design limit.

This repeated overstress accumulates in three places: permanent deformation of the seal grooves, loosening of the cap connection and micro-crack formation in the body. The result is usually seen a few seasons later as a permanent leak, and because of the long time between cause and effect, the real cause is not noticed.

That is why, with repeated seal failures, you should question not only seal quality but also whether the cylinder's pressure rating suits the system.

Choosing a Higher Rating Than Needed

In the opposite case there is no safety problem, but other costs arise. A cylinder with a thicker body is heavier; this weight puts extra load on the structure it is attached to. It also costs more and may need more installation space.

More importantly, a high pressure rating does not compensate for an insufficient bore. Force is pressure times area; even if the cylinder can withstand a high pressure, no extra force is gained if the system does not produce that pressure.

Pressure Spikes and the Safety Margin

The pressure measured in a system is not always constant. Short pressure spikes occur at moments of direction change, sudden stops and when a load drops. These spikes can go well above the normal working pressure seen on the gauge.

These spikes must be taken into account when choosing a pressure rating. Choosing a cylinder exactly equal to system pressure means working at the limit because of these spikes. So a rating whose working pressure is somewhat above system pressure is preferred.

It is also possible to reduce the severity of the spikes. Using the control lever gradually rather than abruptly, not dropping loads hard and using a shock-absorbing element where needed all limit these spikes.

Where Do You Find the Pressure Rating?

Pressure information is usually on the label or embossed stamp on the cylinder body. If the label cannot be read, it can be found through the manufacturer's catalogue or product code. System pressure, on the other hand, is found in the hydraulics section of the tractor's operator manual or in the relief valve setting.
